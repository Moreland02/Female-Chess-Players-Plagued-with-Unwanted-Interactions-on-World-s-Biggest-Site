Female Chess Players Plagued with Unwanted Interactions on World’s Biggest Site

I played 200 games of chess on Chess.com, and found I was five times likelier to receive interaction from my opponents when I played as a woman than when I played as a man. Other female players say they experience the same unwanted interactions.

Megan Moreland • August, 2026

Hannah

Nineteen-year-old Hannah Kassam, a Londoner attending university in Canada, was six when she fell in love with chess.
“My dad started teaching me the basics of chess and then my school started having chess lessons every other week. That’s when I fell in love with the game.”
But early on, she noticed differences in how her all-female team were treated at tournaments.
“Boys kind of assumed they’d win against us; they had a bit of an attitude. Sometimes in between games you have group training with some of the coaches, and I’d get talked over a lot.”

In recent years, discussions of sexism have become more common in chess circles, with professional players making headlines for hitting out at sexist comments. In 2023, chess had its own #MeToo movement after Woman Grandmaster Jennifer Shahade accused Grandmaster Alejandro Ramirez of sexual assault, prompting over 100 female players, both professional and casual, to sign an open letter denouncing sexism and sexual assaults in chess. Since then, Grandmaster Divya Deshmukh made headlines when she said audiences cared more about how she dressed than her play. And Grandmaster Bobby Fischer’s infamous quotes of the 1960s still haunt discussions of women in chess: “They're all weak, all women. They're stupid compared to men.”

Learn about titles

Titles denote exceptional skill. The highest title a person can receive is Grandmaster and requires a minimum rating of 2500. Women can be Grandmasters but can also gain the separate title of Woman Grandmaster, which requires a slightly lower rating of 2400.

Alice

When 24-year-old Zuzanna Adamiec, from Skierniewice in Poland, added a picture of herself to her Chess.com profile, she immediately noticed changes in her opponents’ behaviour.
“One of the first games I played with a profile picture that shows I am a woman, my opponent was winning. And then when he had me in an impossible position, he resigned and said he doesn’t want to ‘discourage’ me,” she said.
“When I was lower rated, there was this situation very often where people were telling me to go to the kitchen so I could cook.”

Twenty-one-year-old student Alice Merati, who lives in Brighton, doesn’t like to include pictures of herself on her profile: “I would probably feel a bit weird having my face be like a young girl on there and having them [opponents] know that. Because pretty much all the people I play are all guys.”
“I had a phase where I had a prompt on my Hinge dating profile that was a chess one, so at one point I was playing lots and lots of guys from Hinge on Chess.com. And a lot of them during the game would send quite patronising messages like ‘oh, don’t worry sweetie’. Or when I would lose or whatever they would send patronising messages on Hinge. It kind of annoyed me. I took away the prompt from my Hinge.”

During the Covid-19 pandemic and following the 2020 release of Netflix’s The Queen’s Gambit, online chess numbers skyrocketed. In 2020, Chess.com went from 35 to 70 million users. By 2022, it was 100 million.
On Chess.com, users can play against friends or strangers, or complete lessons with chess ‘coaches’: bots designed to teach the strategies of the game. It boasts 9.7 million users a day.
Founder of London chess club Casual Chess, Amanda Ross, said this has been important for women’s participation in a historically male-dominated sport: “More women begin chess online than any other way at the moment.”
But Ross says women face unique challenges in these online spaces: “Women are considered a novelty, and a target for romantic attention – usually very unwanted. There can be a flood of online harassment.”

But how much more attention do women receive on chess sites? I investigated with two dummy accounts on Chess.com.

They are both made-up characters, with a rating of about 950. A player’s rating is calculated by Chess.com and indicates their skill level in the game, with higher ratings denoting higher skill.

Learn about ratings

A player’s rating represents their skill level. Chess.com uses the Glicko rating system to calculate a player’s skill based on wins and losses, which cause your rating to increase or decrease respectively.

For 20 days, I played five games a day on each account, for a total of 100 games per account.

Overall, the female account received...

8x more friend requests

3.5x more messages

7x more challenges

4x more profile views

25% of these were from players ‘Chloe’ hadn’t even played.

“So bad Chloe,” one opponent said, referring to my play. “May I have your Instagram?”
“Wow,” said another.
But most of the comments were simply greetings like ‘hello’. ‘Jack’s’ opponents never initiated a conversation in this way.
Adamiec noticed something similar when she changed from a gender-neutral profile picture to one of herself: “people just start conversations like ‘how long have you been playing chess?’”

In chess forums, women have long discussed similar treatment, and describe marriage proposals, floods of friend requests, or threats of rape and murder during online matches.
A common response from forum users is to suggest that women simply change their profile picture. Wipe away any trace of their gender.
“If you want a simple fix, that does work,” Merati said. “But that’s telling the girl to change their actions rather than telling the guy to change their actions, which is obviously what should be happening instead.”
Kassam agrees: “I think it’s a classic example of victim blaming. It implies a woman who has a profile picture somehow deserves to be harassed.”

Journalist Kristina Safonova has come face to face with this in her own investigation into the chess world: “Some of the male interviewees said that women chess players provoke abusive posts by publishing their photos online.” In 2022, Safonova, working for Latvian news outlet Meduza, published an investigation into the anonymous figure who had been sending used condoms and pornographic magazine clippings to female chess players for ten years. Safonova told me she struggled to believe the story at first and the fact this had been going on for years: “Everybody in the chess community knew about it? Impossible! But it was true.” Her investigation unmasked the perpetrator as Andrejs Strebkovs, an ex-International Master. His title was revoked following the Meduza investigation.

For years, Strebkovs had harassed female players online, including on a chess forum that went largely underground following the Meduza investigation. Though the mainstream forum was dedicated to practical chess resources, threads emerged that were dedicated to misogynistic abuse. There, Strebkovs posted images of female players with rude and offensive comments.
But his posts went beyond the ‘Peshka’ forum.
Safonova said Strebkov made “abusive posts and comments not only on Peshka but also on the [Russian] social network VKontakte.”
She said: “Strebkovs published erotic stories about the chess world on the amateur writers' website ‘Proza.ru’. So there was a wide range of platforms available, and his content wasn't removed from any of them.”
Journalist Lilia Yapparova, who worked with Safonova on the investigation, thinks the results of my two-profile experiment on Chess.com speak to “the culture of social media in general, not chess.”
“Peshka wasn't designed for abuse,” Yapparova said, “but it had structural vulnerabilities: anonymity without accountability, minimal moderation, and community culture that discouraged reporting. Those conditions exist on larger platforms too.”

Ross thinks these conditions are visible on chess-related sites: “The websites aren't robust enough on anti-harassment. Women tend to either retire or learn to use lichess.org with a gender-neutral photo or screenname.” Lichess, another popular website for learning chess, places less of an emphasis on social features than Chess.com, focusing more on game analysis and chess lessons built and shared by users.
But the differences in how women are treated go beyond harassment.
Merati thinks that men see losing to her as more insulting than losing to another man. “I play with my housemate who’s a guy a lot. He’ll be grumpy for the rest of the day if I beat him at chess, whereas I’m fine when I lose.”
Many professional female players have described facing poor sportsmanship in offline play. In 2001, Grandmaster Judit Polgar spoke about her sister, Susan Polgar, also a Grandmaster, to the New York Times. "[She] said she never won against a healthy man. After the game, there was always an excuse: 'I had a headache. I had a stomach ache'."
A 2023 study from King’s College London suggests this issue persists over two decades later. The study found that, when losing, men played about 8% more moves before resigning to female opponents compared with male opponents.
This seems to translate to online play. In my own investigation, Chess.com opponents resigned to ‘Jack’ more often than to ‘Chloe’. They were also on average an extra 3 points down before resigning to ‘Chloe’.
Kassam thinks this is significant: “Chess pieces have different values. So, when you talk about having a three-point gap, it’s that difference in material that will give one side an advantage to finish the game faster.”

At the end of every interview I conducted with a chess player, I asked if they were surprised by the results of my investigation. All of them said no.

“I would say that I am disappointed but not surprised,” Kassam said. “Women face harassment and uncalled-for attention in virtually every aspect of our lives, so chess is no different.”

“I am not at all surprised by these results,” Adamiec said. “But that’s a ginormous difference [in interaction]. Wow.”

The only thing Merati was surprised by was that ‘Chloe’ received more rematch requests: “In my head guys would be less likely to want to play girls.”

Chess.com provides a button to report explicit sexual harassment, and a Community Policy that states the site “will not tolerate racism, sexism, bigotry, or violent threats”. But Kassam says that in general it’s difficult to speak up about sexist abuse in chess because of people’s reactions.
“Honestly, I think probably one of the biggest reactions [from other people] is being belittled, being talked down to, being told ‘it’s really not that big of a deal’,” she said. “You know, you get like a really abusive message in your chat while playing online, and they just say ‘well, disable your chat’, or ‘just focus on the game’.”
The number of women playing chess is rising – but slowly. According to the FIDE Women in Chess Commission, female participation sits at 16.5% of all active players, seeing just a 0.5-point increase since 2023.
A slow increase in female players may change the environment women face in chess, online and offline. But in the meantime, for players like Kassam, the struggle inspires them to keep playing.
“Every time I’d go and play in a tournament, the organisers would always talk about how there’s so few girls in chess. That – you know – past the age of nine, past the age of eleven, 85%, 95% of those women would drop out.
“When I was younger it was those statistics being read out that really pushed me and really motivated me to keep going for as long as I could.”
Chess.com did not reply to attempts to contact them about this investigation.

Learn about the methodology

I set up two Chess.com accounts in May, 2026 and played ‘set-up’ games until they had similar starting ratings: 898 (Chloe) and 866 (Jack). These two ratings are likely to be paired with a similar pool of rated players.
Data collection only began after the set-up period was finished.
On both accounts, I played for ten days in the morning and ten days in the afternoon within set three-hour timeframes. Aborted games – where players cancel the game before making a move – were not counted as one of the day’s games.
I played to the best of my ability. I did not attempt to keep the accounts’ ratings exactly the same, allowing them to fluctuate naturally in the same relative bracket of 950 – 1000.
Results were analysed by raw count of interaction types. When opponents sent messages or friend requests, these were not responded to.
